SECTION 02 · ABOUT

Sixty years old. Alive, calm, and done explaining myself.

The photo above was taken this year. The face is calm because the life finally is. I built this memorial while I'm still here to enjoy it — most people get theirs too late.

I won't dress it up: my life was shit until my mid-fifties. A dysfunctional middle-class family, years in America, two marriages I never should have made, and regrets stacked like firewood. The full record is down this page in The Road Here — I've stopped hiding it. That's part of the peace. And these days I'm having a blast: playing with AI, building cool little websites exactly like this one.

My life now is small in the best possible way: my own space, my own routine, my own company — which, it turns out, is excellent. I answer to no one. I perform for no one. I wake up and the day belongs entirely to me, and I have never once found that lonely.

"Peace isn't something they give you. It's what's left when you stop letting them in."

If you're reading this and you're still in the middle of your own noise — hang in there. Sixty isn't a deadline. It's a clearing. And on the other side of all that rubbish is the best company you'll ever keep: your own.

SECTION 03 · THE ROAD HERE · CAUTION: REGRETS AHEAD

Fifty-five years of wrong turns.

Before the peace, there was the life I didn't choose carefully. It goes on the record here — not for sympathy, but because a memorial that leaves out the truth isn't a memorial. It's advertising.

CH. 01 — THE FAMILY I CAME FROM

Dysfunctional, middle-class, and very good at pretending.

The kind of family where everything looks fine from the street and it's a cold little war behind the front door. Nobody talked. Everybody kept score. Affection was rationed, criticism wasn't, and "what people think" was treated like a religion.

I learned young to keep my mouth shut, keep my head down, and carry things I had no business carrying. That wasn't a childhood. It was training — and it took me decades to untrain.

Middle-class doesn't mean it wasn't a mess. It just means the mess had curtains.

CH. 02 — THE AMERICAN YEARS

I crossed an ocean thinking a new map would make a new man.

Then came the USA. Years of it. I lived the thing they advertise — the long roads, the big sky, the fresh start. Here's the truth about fresh starts: you land with yourself. The accent changes, the scenery changes, the hole doesn't.

I don't disown those years. They were mine, and some of it was good. But I spent a lot of them running, and I called it living because it was easier than admitting what it was.

America didn't fix me. Nothing a passport can buy ever does.

CH. 03 — THE TWO MARRIAGES

Married twice. Both times for totally ridiculous reasons.

Marriage number one: a sexy lady from Hillsboro, Oregon. About eight and a half years. If you're asking why I married her, the honest answer is — ridiculous reasons. Not evil reasons. Ridiculous ones. At the time I told myself it was love, or timing, or just what a man's supposed to do. Sixty gives you x-ray vision for your own bullshit.

Marriage number two: a lady from Tacoma, Washington. I'll call it exactly what it was — she was the spawn of Satan. For a couple of years I lived like white trailer trash. The kind of low you don't see coming and can't properly explain afterward.

I've made many bad choices in my life, and they shaped me into this — this "meat sack" that looks human, sitting here at sixty. But a man who admits his choices made him is a man who knows the next choice can remake him.

Two marriages. Both entered for totally ridiculous reasons. Both are now exhibits, not addresses.

CH. 04 — THE WISH

If there was a time machine, I know exactly where I'd park it.

I have many, many regrets — enough to fill this page twice. If there was a machine, I'd go back to both altars and shake myself by the collar. Smarter choices, made with my own help — not fate's, not theirs, mine. I'd change my life into something new.

Never wanted the life I was dealt. That goes on the record once, plainly: never wanted it. But sixty teaches you something else — the hand you're dealt is not the hand you have to finish with.

And here's the funny part. I never got a time machine, but I found the next best thing. These days I play with AI, building cool little websites like this one, and I'm having a blast. I can't redo the past — but I can build something new, one page at a time. That's the machine. This is it.

No time machine found. Building the new life with AI instead.

The letter I never sent.

There's a daughter. I don't really know her. I don't know her days, her people, what she's like when she laughs — I don't even know if she's married. That's the sentence that costs the most to write, and I'm writing it anyway.

I'm not dressing it up, and I'm not blaming anyone for the distance — least of all her. Some of the silence was done to me. A lot of it was done by me. At sixty, a man can finally tell the difference, and he has to own his share.

Every quiet morning I've got now, there's a quiet underneath it with her name on it. That one doesn't heal neat. I don't expect it to. I just refuse to pretend it isn't there.

And if she ever reads this: I'm not asking you for anything. I'm only telling you the door isn't locked. It was never locked.

2002 — 2007 · KATAMATITE, VICTORIA

The Reason My Mum Had Her Heart Broken

I had two older brothers. One was kept in the dark — not because he was stupid, but because of his cynical judgement. He saw things through a lens that made everything worse, so we stopped telling him things. That wasn't kindness. That was cowardice, and it cost us.

The other brother had his head screwed on backwards. That's not an insult — that's a medical diagnosis of how he processed the world. Everything came out wrong. Every conversation was a minefield of misunderstandings and bad intentions he didn't even know he had.

Neither were my friend. Neither were even a brother to me. They were two people I shared DNA with and a childhood with, and that was the end of it. When Mum's heart broke — when the family finally cracked open and showed what it really was — they were nowhere. Not because they were busy. Because they were never really there to begin with.

Katamatite was supposed to be home. It was just another place where I learned that blood doesn't make family. Presence does. And neither of them showed up.
